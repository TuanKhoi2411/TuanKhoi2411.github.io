01 · Context

A portfolio built through a crisis

Background

The platform connected borrowers with individual investors between 2007 and 2014, a window spanning the 2008 financial crisis and recovery. The dates provide economic context, but the portfolio data alone does not prove a macroeconomic cause for each movement.

2007-2008
Crisis
Small book, high severity. Recovery-defined bad rate at 15.9%.
2009-2010
Recovery
Best economics of the period. ROI peaks at +11.4%.
2011-2013
Scale-up
Volume surges; raw default rate rises from 9.3% to 15.0%.
2014
Reckoning
Raw default rate eases to 11.3%, but ROI falls to -36.4%.

Objectives of the analysis

  • 01
    Behaviour across cycles

    Track how repayment and borrowing behaviour shift between crisis, recovery and expansion.

  • 02
    Real risk drivers

    Isolate the factors that actually drive credit risk, from borrower profile and loan characteristics.

  • 03
    A sharper scorecard

    Refine the credit scoring framework to improve approvals, portfolio performance and default control.

Scope: ~140K loans · ~$2B funded · 4 report pages · 20 KPI cards

Approach

  • Deep exploration of lending data to surface borrower segments and behaviour before, during and after the crisis.
  • Decision tree models to rank risk drivers and isolate the borrower groups most exposed to default.

Challenges

  • Only 35.98% of applications are fully verified; Source Verified and Not Verified require separate treatment.
  • The current dashboard describes associations, not causal effects; policy changes should be validated against outcomes.

03 · Definitions

Three types of loan behaviour

Outcome is defined by recovery, not only by status label. This is what the Loan label slicer drives across all four pages.

Good loans

> 70%
  • Loans with status Fully Paid, regardless of the calculated recovery ratio.
  • Charged-off, defaulted or late loans that still recover above 70%.
  • Current loans that have passed more than 40% of their term.

Neutral loans

Otherwise
  • Loans currently in the grace period.
  • Adverse-status loans not captured by the strict <40% bad or >70% good rules.
  • Early current loans remain neutral until they pass 40% of term.

Bad loans

< 40%
  • Charged-off, defaulted or late loans with a recovery rate below 40%.
  • They form the numerator of the bad-rate KPI; seasoned applications form its denominator.
  • The current model contains 2,969 seasoned bad loans.
Why this matters for the KPI set

Because bad is defined by recovery severity, a falling default rate does not automatically mean a healthier book. The dashboard therefore pairs every quality KPI with a recovery KPI, which is exactly where the 2013–2014 story breaks.

04 · Measurement

Two clocks in one dashboard

The model carries two different measure families. Reading a number from the wrong one is the easiest way to draw the wrong conclusion.

The seasoned lens · pages 1 to 3

Every quality KPI is prefixed Seasoned: Seasoned Loans, Seasoned Bad Rate, Seasoned Good Rate, Seasoned Recovery, Seasoning Coverage.

  • Denominator is restricted to loans mature enough to judge, so a young cohort cannot dilute the ratio.
  • Outcome is defined by recovery severity, bad means recovery under 40%, not simply a status label.
  • Answers the question: is the credit we wrote any good?

The raw lens · page 4

Pricing KPIs use unfiltered measures: Raw Default Rate, Portfolio ROI, Average Loan Amount, Average Interest Rate, Income Verification Rate.

  • Denominator is the whole book, including loans still current and far from maturity.
  • Outcome is status frequency at a point in time, plus the money actually returned.
  • Answers the question: did we price and earn on what we wrote?

Why the two headline trends point in opposite directions

  • Raw default rate rises from 0.0% in 2007 to 15.0% in 2013, then eases to 11.3% in 2014. It uses status and the whole book.
  • The seasoned lens reports 52,209 eligible loans, 5.7% bad, 86.7% good and 84.2% recovery across the portfolio.
  • No 2014 loan passes the fixed 40% seasoning gate, so a 2014 seasoned bad rate would be invalid and is intentionally not shown.

Monthly KPI, annual chart

Every KPI card carries a monthly sparkline and a MoM or variance-in-pp delta, while the charts underneath are plotted by year. A KPI can therefore be red month-over-month inside an improving annual trend. Check the Seasoning Coverage KPI before trusting any bad rate.

Volume strategy

Grades B and C dominate volume, but their seasoned bad rates remain 3.9% and 6.7%. Volume and risk must be read separately.

Where it deteriorates

Grade D contains 696 seasoned bad loans against 6,187 good, a 9.1% bad rate. E, F and G rise further to 13.2%, 16.8% and 21.8%.

Shift after 2010

The monotonic grade pattern is the decision signal: risk roughly doubles from B to D and more than doubles again from D to G.

Action on page 3: tighten approval for weak grades

Grade is not a reporting dimension here, it is a decision variable. Constraining D and below while protecting A–C volume is the single cleanest lever in the book.

The divergence that matters

Default rate Portfolio ROI
Exact annual measures from the model: raw default rate peaks at 15.0% in 2013 and ends at 11.3% in 2014; ROI peaks at +11.4% in 2010 and ends at -36.4%.

Growth without pricing discipline

  • Average loan size surged 84%, from $8.1K in 2007 to $14.9K in 2014: clear appetite for larger tickets.
  • Interest rates did not hold steady, they peaked at 14.5% in 2013 before settling at 13.8%.
  • That reads as reactive pricing: raising price after quality had already deteriorated.

Severity, not frequency

Raw default frequency eased from its 15.0% peak in 2013 to 11.3% in 2014, while weighted recovery fell from 71.2% to 48.2%. ROI ended at -36.4%: severity overwhelmed the frequency improvement.

10 · Page 3 — Risk Drivers & Actions

From drivers to approval rules

PBIX · Page 03 · Risk Drivers

Model discipline

The model reports AUC 0.698 and Gini 0.396. That is useful but moderate discrimination, so rules should augment—not replace—underwriting judgment.

Feature importance

Pricing and grade lead the driver ranking, ahead of borrower attributes. Risk is priced into the loan terms before the borrower profile adds much extra signal.

Term step-up 36M → 60M

Seasoned bad rate rises from 4.7% at 36 months to 12.8% at 60 months; recovery falls from 87.6% to 70.9%. Term is a risk decision.

High-risk rule cohorts

The deployed high-risk rule isolates 183 seasoned loans at a 15.8% bad rate and 2.79x lift. Low-recovery exposure totals $26.4M.

The action set the dashboard argues for

  • Tighten approval for weak grades, D and below, while keeping A–C volume intact.
  • Constrain 60-month terms for anything other than verified, low-DTI applicants.
  • Decline or re-price the high-risk rule cohorts instead of absorbing them at book pricing.
  • Combine signals: grade plus DTI plus purpose, never one factor in isolation.

Why combinations beat single cuts

The deployed rule combines income below $30K, interest above 17% and grade E/F/G. Together these conditions produce a 15.8% seasoned bad rate and 2.79x lift, making the rule directly testable as decision policy.

11 · Coverage check

Two verified cuts, two open questions

Risk by income band

Seasoned bad rate declines with income: 7.8% low, 6.0% medium and 3.6% high. The medium band carries 97.5K applications, so it remains the largest absolute underwriting workload.

Geography

A State slicer sits on all four pages and DimState is fully modelled, yet no state-level finding was produced. If loss concentrates in a handful of states, state becomes a pricing and exposure lever rather than a filter.

Term recovery trade-off

Page 3 pairs Term Risk Step-Up with Term Recovery Trade-off. The verified gap is +8.1 pp: 12.8% bad at 60M versus 4.7% at 36M.

Borrower stress signals

Avg Delinquency, Public Record Rate and Avg DTI each have a monthly sparkline and a delta, but no chart of their own. They are the natural inputs for the early-warning model in recommendation 3.

What the deck does cover

  • The deck now distinguishes status, recovery label and seasoning, so each metric uses the same definition as the report.
  • Grade, purpose, income, home ownership, term, verification, status and date are covered; State remains an open cut.

What blocks the last mile

  • State-level loss concentration and the causal effect of verification are not established by the current pages.
  • Those questions require a controlled analysis; the deck does not infer them from simple volume differences.

12 · Synthesis

Four structural insights

INSIGHT 01

Risk is structurally concentrated, not random

Credit risk clusters in mid-to-low grades, B to D, rather than spreading evenly. Grade D alone contributes a disproportionate share of bad loans, which points at underwriting weakness in the lower tiers rather than bad luck.

Grade D: 696 seasoned bad vs 6,187 good
INSIGHT 02

Product mix drives systemic underperformance

Debt consolidation dominates origination and contributes 55.7% of seasoned bad loans. Its rate is near the portfolio average; concentration is driven by volume, not an unusually high purpose rate.

55.7% of seasoned bad loans, one purpose
INSIGHT 03

Income quality, not income level, is the core issue

The borrower base concentrates in the medium income band while only 35.98% is fully verified. Verification is a plausible underwriting lever, but its causal effect on recovery is not established here.

Only 35.98% fully verified
INSIGHT 04

Severity beat frequency

Default rates stabilised and improved, yet recovery rates collapsed and ROI fell sharply. Loss severity, not default frequency, became the primary driver of value erosion in 2013 and 2014.

ROI +11.4% (2010) → -36.4% (2014)

13 · Recommendation

Four moves to rebuild the return

1

Rebalance risk at the portfolio entry point

  • Shift lending focus toward low and medium risk segments, grades A to C, while strictly constraining grade D exposure.
  • Introduce risk-adjusted approval rules combining credit grade, DTI and loan purpose instead of evaluating each factor in isolation.
2

Redesign the debt consolidation strategy

  • Treat debt consolidation as a controlled product, not a growth driver.
  • Approve selectively on verified income, evidence of repayment capacity improvement and behavioural signals, not volume targets.
3

Strengthen data integrity and early risk detection

  • Enforce high verification standards for income-sensitive segments and embed verification depth directly into credit scoring.
  • Deploy early-warning models to flag repayment stress within the first months of loan life.
4

Build a sustainable credit ecosystem

  • Standardise credit data architecture to support behavioural analytics and long-term model learning.
  • Complement controls with borrower financial education, especially for first-time and low-income customers.
Sequencing

Moves 1 and 3 protect the next cohort and can start immediately inside the existing dashboard. Move 2 resets the product economics. Move 4 is what keeps the model improving instead of re-learning the same lesson in the next cycle.
